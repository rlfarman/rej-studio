REJ Studio is a design tool for a specific technique — **RNA
End-Joining** (REJ) — and most of the parameters in the app only make
sense once you know what that technique is trying to do. This page
gives you the background. If you already know all this, skip to the
[tutorial](/docs/tutorials/getting-started).

## The technique in one paragraph

RNA End-Joining is a method for producing a full-length protein from
**two separate transcripts** that are joined inside the cell, rather
than from one full-length mRNA. You design a coding sequence, divide
it into a 5′ half and a 3′ half at a WGGW motif, deliver each half as
its own transcript, and rely on the REJ reaction to rejoin them. The
encoded protein is identical to the original; only the delivery is
split.

## Why split a coding sequence at all?

A few reasons people care about this:

- **Payload size.** Delivery vectors cap how much nucleic acid can fit
  in a single particle. AAV, for example, packages about 4.7 kb of
  single-stranded DNA (ITRs, promoter, CDS, poly-A and all). Splitting
  a large CDS across two particles gets you past that ceiling without
  redesigning the protein.
- **Modularity.** Each half can be optimized, expressed, or replaced
  independently — useful during development, and especially useful
  when you want to swap one half for a variant.
- **Safety.** A split design is only productive when both halves are
  delivered to the same cell, which is useful for constructs where
  uncontrolled single-fragment expression is a concern.

None of these concerns matter if the protein doesn't come out intact.
The whole point of sequence design for REJ is to make sure the split
happens where the two halves can actually rejoin, and that each half
behaves as a well-formed transcript on its own.

## What makes a REJ-ready sequence different from a normal CDS?

Two requirements:

1. **The join has to sit at a WGGW motif.** `WGGW` (where `W` = A or T,
   i.e. `[AT]GG[AT]`) is the four-base motif the REJ reaction targets
   at the junction. The split point needs to fall within a WGGW,
   ideally near the middle of the CDS so the two halves are balanced.
   See [the glossary](/docs/reference/glossary#wggw-motif) for the full
   definition.
2. **Each fragment has to behave like a proper transcript on its own.**
   That means no cryptic splice sites silently excising parts of the
   CDS, no repeat-heavy regions that destabilize synthesis, controlled
   GC content, and — optionally — a stimulatory-intron marker where an
   intron can be placed to enhance expression. These are the knobs in
   the [design tool](/docs/guides/design-sequence).

Everything REJ Studio does is in service of those two requirements:
find a good WGGW-anchored split, then clean up each half so it
expresses reliably.

## Where REJ Studio fits

REJ Studio doesn't run the end-joining experiment. It's the **design
stage** — the part where you pick a gene, inspect its transcripts, let
the splitter find a balanced WGGW cut, run the optimizer over each
fragment, and download the result. You take the downloaded sequences
into your synthesis + delivery pipeline from there.

Think of it as a constraint-aware codon optimizer that understands the
REJ-specific requirements (WGGW join, fragment balance, splice
hygiene), not a general-purpose molecular biology platform.

## What REJ Studio does _not_ do

- **No wet-lab validation.** The optimizer is a constraint solver;
  biology is not a constraint solver. Every sequence you design here
  should be tested before you trust it.
- **No claim about the specific REJ chemistry.** The tool treats WGGW
  as the join-site requirement and is agnostic about the underlying
  enzymology — it just ensures the sequence meets the structural
  requirements.
- **No prediction of expression, mRNA stability, folding, rejoining
  efficiency, or immunogenicity.** Those are downstream questions REJ
  Studio doesn't attempt to answer.
